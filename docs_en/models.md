## 0. Frames, symbols and signs

![Frames and sign convention](images/derivation_frames.png)

A right-handed frame fixed to the body: $x$ forward, $y$ to the left, $z$ up.
The yaw angle $\psi$ is measured from the global $X$ axis, positive
counter-clockwise, and the yaw rate is $r = \dot\psi$. A positive steer angle
$\delta$ therefore turns left and produces $r > 0$.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $m$, $I_z$ | mass, yaw inertia about the CoG |
| $l_f$, $l_r$, $L = l_f + l_r$ | CoG to front axle, to rear axle, wheelbase |
| $T_f$, $T_r$ | front and rear track |
| $h$ | CoG height |
| $C_f$, $C_r$ | cornering stiffness **per axle** $[\mathrm{N/rad}]$ |
| $\mu$ | tire-road friction coefficient |
| $v_x$, $v_y$ | body-frame velocity components at the CoG |
| $\beta$ | body slip angle, $\tan\beta = v_y/v_x$ |
| $\alpha$ | tire slip angle |

The one relation every model shares is the transformation of the body velocity
into the global frame. With the rotation matrix $R(\psi)$,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot X \\
\dot Y
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\psi & -\sin\psi \\
\sin\psi & \cos\psi
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
v_x \\
v_y
\end{bmatrix}
$$

so $\dot X = v_x\cos\psi - v_y\sin\psi$ and $\dot Y = v_x\sin\psi + v_y\cos\psi$.
Models that assume $v_y = 0$ collapse this to $\dot X = v\cos\psi$,
$\dot Y = v\sin\psi$.

**Body-frame acceleration.** Differentiating the global velocity and rotating
back gives the term every dynamic model needs. Writing
$\mathbf{v} = (v_x, v_y)$ and $\boldsymbol\omega = r\ \hat z$,

$$
\mathbf{a}_{\text{body}} = \dot{\mathbf{v}} + \boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf{v}
= (\dot v_x - r v_y,\ \dot v_y + r v_x)
$$

The cross-product terms are not forces; they appear because the frame rotates.
Every equation in sections 4, 5 and 7 starts from this.

## 1. Unicycle / differential drive

![Differential drive geometry](images/derivation_unicycle.png)

**Assumption.** The wheels roll without slipping, so the body has no velocity
along the wheel axis: $v_y = 0$.

**Derivation.** A rigid body in the plane rotates about its instantaneous
centre of rotation (ICR). If the body centre is a distance $R$ from the ICR and
the body turns at $\omega$, then by definition

$$
v = \omega R
$$

The two wheels sit on the same axle, a distance $b/2$ either side of the
centre, so their own radii are $R \mp b/2$. Rolling without slipping, the
contact speed of each wheel is its rolling speed $R_w\omega_l$, $R_w\omega_r$:

$$
R_w \omega_l = \omega\left(R - \frac{b}{2}\right),
\qquad
R_w \omega_r = \omega\left(R + \frac{b}{2}\right)
$$

Adding the two eliminates $b$ and gives $R_w(\omega_r + \omega_l) = 2\omega R = 2v$.
Subtracting them eliminates $R$ and gives $R_w(\omega_r - \omega_l) = \omega b$.

**Result.**

$$
v = \frac{R_w(\omega_r + \omega_l)}{2},
\qquad
\omega = \frac{R_w(\omega_r - \omega_l)}{b}
$$

and with $v_y = 0$ the pose follows from section 0:

$$
\dot x = v\cos\psi,
\qquad
\dot y = v\sin\psi,
\qquad
\dot\psi = \omega
$$

**Why this is the wrong model for a car.** Set $\omega_r = -\omega_l$. Then
$v = 0$ while $\omega \ne 0$, so $R = v/\omega = 0$: the vehicle spins on the
spot. Nothing in the derivation bounds $R$ from below, because nothing tied the
two wheels to a shared steering geometry. Use this as a planner-side
abstraction, or for skid-steer platforms only.

## 2. Ackermann geometry

![Ackermann geometry](images/derivation_ackermann.png)

**Assumption.** Every wheel rolls without side slip, so each wheel's velocity
lies in its own plane. A wheel's velocity is perpendicular to the line joining
it to the ICR, so **every wheel plane must be perpendicular to its own radius
from the ICR**. That single statement is the whole of Ackermann geometry.

**Where the ICR must lie.** The rear wheels are not steered, so their planes
point along $x$; their radii must therefore point along $y$. The ICR is thus on
the extension of the rear axle. Call its distance from the rear axle centre $R$;
taking that centre as the origin, the ICR is at $(0, R)$.

**Front wheels.** The front left wheel sits at $(L, +T_f/2)$ relative to the
rear axle centre. The vector from that wheel to the ICR is
$(-L,\ R - T_f/2)$. Its wheel plane must be perpendicular to this, which fixes
the steer angle through

$$
\tan\delta_l = \frac{L}{R - T_f/2}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\cot\delta_l = \frac{R - T_f/2}{L}
$$

The front right wheel is at $(L, -T_f/2)$, giving $\cot\delta_r = (R + T_f/2)/L$.

**Result — the ideal Ackermann condition.** Subtracting the two removes $R$:

$$
\cot\delta_{\text{outer}} - \cot\delta_{\text{inner}} = \frac{T_f}{L}
$$

A single-track (bicycle) equivalent uses $\cot\delta = R/L$, so the same pair
can be written around it:

$$
\cot\delta_l = \cot\delta - \frac{T_f}{2L},
\qquad
\cot\delta_r = \cot\delta + \frac{T_f}{2L}
$$

which is what `ackermann.hpp` evaluates. A real rack does not achieve this
exactly, so the library blends linearly toward parallel steering with the ratio
$k$ (`ackermann_ratio`):

$$
\delta_i = \delta + k\ (\delta_{i,\text{ideal}} - \delta)
$$

with $k = 1$ ideal Ackermann and $k = 0$ parallel steering. Typical racks sit at
60–80 %; `ackermannError()` returns the resulting deviation of the outer wheel.

**Wheel speeds.** For a rigid body, the velocity of the point at body position
$(x_p, y_p)$ is $\mathbf{v}_p = \mathbf{v} + \boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf{r}_p$,
that is

$$
\mathbf{v}_p = (v_x - r\ y_p,\ \ v_y + r\ x_p)
$$

A wheel speed sensor measures the component **along the wheel plane**. For the
rear wheels ($x_p = 0$, plane along $x$) that is simply the $x$ component:

$$
v_{rl} = v - \frac{T_r}{2} r,
\qquad
v_{rr} = v + \frac{T_r}{2} r
$$

For the front wheels ($x_p = L$, plane at $\delta$) project
$(v \mp T_f r/2,\ L r)$ onto $(\cos\delta,\ \sin\delta)$:

$$
v_{fl} = \left(v - \frac{T_f}{2} r\right)\cos\delta_l + L r \sin\delta_l,
\qquad
v_{fr} = \left(v + \frac{T_f}{2} r\right)\cos\delta_r + L r \sin\delta_r
$$

## 3. Kinematic bicycle

![Kinematic bicycle](images/derivation_kinematic_bicycle.png)

**Assumption.** Zero tire slip, as in section 2, with the two wheels of each
axle lumped into one. The tires are assumed able to supply whatever lateral
force the path demands.

**Derivation.** Take the rear axle centre as the reference and let its speed be
$v$. The rear wheel is unsteered, so its velocity is along $x$: the rear axle
point has body velocity $(v, 0)$. By the rigid-body relation of section 2 with
$x_p = L$, the front axle point moves at

$$
\mathbf{v}_f = (v,\ L r)
$$

The front wheel rolls without side slip, so $\mathbf{v}_f$ must lie in its own
plane, which is at angle $\delta$:

$$
\tan\delta = \frac{L r}{v}
$$

**Result — rear axle reference.**

$$
\dot x = v\cos\psi,
\qquad
\dot y = v\sin\psi,
\qquad
\dot\psi = \frac{v\tan\delta}{L},
\qquad
\dot v = a
$$

Equivalently $R = L/\tan\delta$, which is the same ICR the previous section
constructed.

**Centre of gravity reference.** The CoG sits $l_r$ ahead of the rear axle, so
its velocity is $(v, l_r r)$ and it does *not* point along the body axis. The
body slip angle follows directly:

$$
\tan\beta = \frac{l_r r}{v} = \frac{l_r \tan\delta}{L}
$$

If $v$ now denotes the **CoG** speed, the rear axle speed is $v\cos\beta$, so
substituting into $\dot\psi = v_{\text{rear}}\tan\delta/L$ gives

$$
\dot x = v\cos(\psi + \beta),
\qquad
\dot y = v\sin(\psi + \beta),
\qquad
\dot\psi = \frac{v\cos\beta\ \tan\delta}{L}
$$

**Front axle reference.** If $v$ is the front wheel speed then the rear axle
speed is $v\cos\delta$, and $\dot\psi = v\cos\delta\tan\delta/L$:

$$
\dot x = v\cos(\psi + \delta),
\qquad
\dot y = v\sin(\psi + \delta),
\qquad
\dot\psi = \frac{v\sin\delta}{L}
$$

The three forms describe the same vehicle; they differ only in which point is
integrated. `ReferencePoint` selects one.

**Where the assumption fails, quantitatively.** In steady cornering the front
axle must carry $F_{yf} = m a_y l_r/L$ (section 4), so the slip angle it
actually needs is $\alpha_f \approx F_{yf}/C_f$:

$$
\alpha_f \approx \frac{m\ l_r}{L\ C_f}\ a_y
$$

For the passenger car preset ($m = 1600$, $l_r = 1.5$, $L = 2.7$,
$C_f = 90\ 000$) this is $9.9 \times 10^{-3}$ rad per $\mathrm{m/s^2}$, so at
$a_y = 0.4\ g$ the neglected slip angle is about $2.2^\circ$ — the same order as
the steer angle itself at moderate radii. That is the origin of the $0.4\ g$
rule of thumb, and why the kinematic model over-predicts yaw rate above it (see
`examples/step_steer.cpp`).

**With a steering actuator.** `KinematicBicycleSteerModel` adds a first order
lag with a rate limit:

$$
\dot\delta = \mathrm{clamp}\left(\frac{\delta_{\text{cmd}} - \delta}{\tau},\ \pm\dot\delta_{\max}\right)
$$

## 4. Linear 2-DOF (lateral) model

![Slip angle definition](images/derivation_slip_angle.png)

**Assumptions.** Constant $v_x$; small angles ($\sin\delta \approx \delta$,
$\cos\delta \approx 1$, $\arctan u \approx u$); linear tires
$F_y = C\alpha$; no load transfer.

**Step 1 — slip angles.** By the rigid-body relation, the lateral velocity is
$v_y + l_f r$ at the front axle and $v_y - l_r r$ at the rear (figure b above).
The slip angle is the angle from the wheel plane to the velocity, so

$$
\alpha_f = \delta - \arctan\frac{v_y + l_f r}{v_x}
\approx \delta - \frac{v_y + l_f r}{v_x},
\qquad
\alpha_r \approx -\frac{v_y - l_r r}{v_x}
$$

**Step 2 — Newton-Euler.** Using the body-frame acceleration from section 0 and
dropping $\sin\delta$ terms,

$$
m(\dot v_y + v_x r) = F_{yf} + F_{yr},
\qquad
I_z \dot r = l_f F_{yf} - l_r F_{yr}
$$

**Step 3 — substitute the linear tires.** With $F_{yf} = C_f\alpha_f$ and
$F_{yr} = C_r\alpha_r$, the lateral equation becomes

$$
m(\dot v_y + v_x r) = C_f\delta - \frac{C_f + C_r}{v_x}v_y -
\frac{l_f C_f - l_r C_r}{v_x}r
$$

and the yaw equation

$$
I_z\dot r = l_f C_f\delta - \frac{l_f C_f - l_r C_r}{v_x}v_y -
\frac{l_f^2 C_f + l_r^2 C_r}{v_x}r
$$

**Result.** Moving $m v_x r$ to the right-hand side and dividing through:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}
\begin{bmatrix}
v_y \\
r
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
-\dfrac{C_f + C_r}{m v_x} & -v_x - \dfrac{l_f C_f - l_r C_r}{m v_x} \\
-\dfrac{l_f C_f - l_r C_r}{I_z v_x} & -\dfrac{l_f^2 C_f + l_r^2 C_r}{I_z v_x}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
v_y \\
r
\end{bmatrix} +
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{C_f}{m} \\
\dfrac{l_f C_f}{I_z}
\end{bmatrix}
\delta
$$

`stateMatrix()` and `inputMatrix()` return exactly this $A$ and $B$, so the
model drops straight into an LQR or MPC design, or into an observer.

### 4.1 Steady state cornering, from a force balance

![Yaw rate gain](images/derivation_handling.png)

The closed-form results in `linear_analysis.hpp` are quicker to get from
equilibrium than from $A$ and $B$. In steady cornering at radius $R$ the yaw
acceleration is zero, so

$$
F_{yf} + F_{yr} = m a_y,
\qquad
l_f F_{yf} - l_r F_{yr} = 0
$$

Solving the pair gives the axle loads shared in inverse proportion to their
distances,

$$
F_{yf} = \frac{m a_y l_r}{L},
\qquad
F_{yr} = \frac{m a_y l_f}{L}
$$

so the slip angles each axle needs are $\alpha_f = m a_y l_r/(L C_f)$ and
$\alpha_r = m a_y l_f/(L C_r)$. The steer angle has to supply the geometric
part $L/R$ **plus** the difference of the two slip angles:

$$
\delta = \frac{L}{R} + \alpha_f - \alpha_r
= \frac{L}{R} + \frac{m}{L}\left(\frac{l_r}{C_f} - \frac{l_f}{C_r}\right)a_y
$$

**Result — the understeer gradient.**

$$
K = \frac{m}{L}\left(\frac{l_r}{C_f} - \frac{l_f}{C_r}\right)
\quad [\mathrm{rad/(m/s^2)}],
\qquad
\delta = \frac{L}{R} + K a_y
$$

Substituting $a_y = v^2/R$ gives $\delta = (L + Kv^2)/R$, and since $r = v/R$:

$$
\frac{r}{\delta} = \frac{v}{L + K v^2},
\qquad
\frac{a_y}{\delta} = \frac{v^2}{L + K v^2}
$$

**Characteristic speed.** For $K > 0$ the gain has a maximum. Differentiating,
$\frac{d}{dv}\frac{v}{L+Kv^2} = 0$ requires $L + Kv^2 = 2Kv^2$, that is

$$
V_{ch} = \sqrt{\frac{L}{K}}
$$

At that speed the gain is $V_{ch}/(2L)$, exactly half the neutral-steer value
$v/L$ — which is the usual definition of $V_{ch}$, recovered here rather than
assumed.

**Critical speed.** For $K < 0$ the denominator reaches zero at

$$
V_{cr} = \sqrt{-\frac{L}{K}}
$$

and the steady-state gain diverges: above it the vehicle is unstable.

**Body slip angle.** At the CoG, $\beta$ is the kinematic part $l_r/R$ reduced
by the slip the rear axle actually runs at:

$$
\beta = \frac{l_r}{R} - \alpha_r
= \frac{1}{R}\left(l_r - \frac{m l_f v^2}{L C_r}\right)
$$

It changes sign at $v = \sqrt{l_r L C_r/(m l_f)}$. Below that speed $\beta > 0$
in a left turn, as the kinematic model always predicts; above it $\beta < 0$.
That sign flip is the clearest single symptom of the kinematic model's validity
limit.

**Neutral steer point and static margin.** Hold the vehicle in pure translation
(no yaw) with a side velocity, so both axles run at the same slip angle
$\alpha$. The axle forces are then $C_f\alpha$ and $C_r\alpha$, and their
resultant acts at the distance behind the front axle where the moments balance,
$x_{NSP}(C_f + C_r)\alpha = L\ C_r\alpha$:

$$
x_{NSP} = \frac{L C_r}{C_f + C_r},
\qquad
SM = \frac{x_{NSP} - l_f}{L}
$$

$SM > 0$ means the neutral steer point is behind the CoG. That is the same
condition as $K > 0$: both reduce to $l_r C_r > l_f C_f$.

**Yaw mode.** The characteristic polynomial of $A$ is
$\lambda^2 - (\mathrm{tr}\ A)\lambda + \det A = 0$. Matching it to the standard
second-order form $\lambda^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n\lambda + \omega_n^2$:

$$
\omega_n = \sqrt{\det A},
\qquad
\zeta = -\frac{\mathrm{tr}\ A}{2\sqrt{\det A}}
$$

## 5. Nonlinear single track (dynamic bicycle)

![Single track free body diagram](images/derivation_single_track_fbd.png)

**Assumptions.** Rigid body, one wheel per axle, planar motion. No small-angle
approximation and no restriction on the tire model. Load transfer is
quasi-static.

**Derivation.** Sum the forces in the body frame, using the rotating-frame
acceleration from section 0. The front lateral force acts perpendicular to the
*steered* wheel plane, so it contributes $-F_{yf}\sin\delta$ along $x$ and
$F_{yf}\cos\delta$ along $y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m(\dot v_x - v_y r) &= F_x - F_{yf}\sin\delta - F_{\text{res}} \\
m(\dot v_y + v_x r) &= F_{yf}\cos\delta + F_{yr} \\
I_z \dot r &= l_f F_{yf}\cos\delta - l_r F_{yr}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Slip angles**, now without linearisation, from the same axle velocities as in
section 4:

$$
\alpha_f = \delta - \arctan\frac{v_y + l_f r}{v_x},
\qquad
\alpha_r = -\arctan\frac{v_y - l_r r}{v_x}
$$

`guardDenominator` puts a floor under $v_x$ so the derivative stays finite at
standstill.

**Longitudinal load transfer.**

![Load transfer](images/derivation_load_transfer.png)

Take moments about the rear contact patch (figure a). The weight $mg$ acts at
$l_r$ ahead of it, the inertial force $m a_x$ acts backwards at height $h$, and
the front axle carries $F_{zf}$ at distance $L$:

$$
F_{zf}L = m g\ l_r - m a_x h
$$

With $F_{zf} + F_{zr} = mg$ the pair follows:

$$
\Delta F_z = \frac{m a_x h}{L},
\qquad
F_{zf} = \frac{m g l_r}{L} - \Delta F_z,
\qquad
F_{zr} = \frac{m g l_f}{L} + \Delta F_z
$$

**Driving resistance.**

$$
F_{\text{res}} = \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_d A\ v_x |v_x|}_{\text{aerodynamic}} +
\underbrace{\mu_{rr}\ m g \tanh\frac{v_x}{0.1}}_{\text{rolling}}
$$

The coefficient $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_d A$ is the parameter `drag_area`
$[\mathrm{N/(m/s)^2}]$ and $\mu_{rr}$ is `rolling_resistance`. The $\tanh$
replaces $\mathrm{sign}(v_x)$ so the force reverses smoothly through standstill
instead of chattering; $0.1$ m/s sets the width of that transition.

## 6. Kinematic / dynamic blending

**The problem.** Both slip angles contain $\arctan(\cdot/v_x)$. As
$v_x \to 0$ the argument diverges, so an arbitrarily small $v_y$ produces a
slip angle approaching $\pm\pi/2$ and therefore a saturated tire force. The
single-track model is not merely inaccurate at walking pace, it is wrong: the
lateral dynamics become stiff and the sign of the force depends on numerical
noise.

**The fix.** Interpolate the *derivatives* between the dynamic solution and a
first-order pull toward the kinematic one, using a blend factor that is 0 below
$v_{lo}$ and 1 above $v_{hi}$:

$$
\lambda = \mathrm{clamp}\left(\frac{|v_x| - v_{lo}}{v_{hi} - v_{lo}},\ 0,\ 1\right)
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot v_y &= \lambda\ \dot v_{y,\text{dyn}} + (1 - \lambda)\frac{v_{y,\text{kin}} - v_y}{\tau} \\
\dot r &= \lambda\ \dot r_{\text{dyn}} + (1 - \lambda)\frac{r_{\text{kin}} - r}{\tau}
\end{aligned}
$$

where the kinematic targets come from section 3,

$$
v_{y,\text{kin}} = v_x \tan\beta_{\text{kin}},
\qquad
r_{\text{kin}} = \frac{v_x \cos\beta_{\text{kin}} \tan\delta}{L}
$$

**Why a first-order pull rather than an assignment.** Setting
$v_y = v_{y,\text{kin}}$ directly would make the state discontinuous whenever
$\delta$ changes. The form $\dot v_y = (v_{y,\text{kin}} - v_y)/\tau$ is a
stable first-order filter whose fixed point *is* the kinematic solution, so the
state converges to it with time constant $\tau$ while remaining differentiable —
which is what the integrator needs. Above $v_{hi}$, $\lambda = 1$ and the model
is bit-for-bit the plain dynamic one (the port test checks this to $10^{-12}$).

## 7. Double track (four wheels)

![Double track](images/derivation_double_track.png)

**Assumptions.** Four contact patches, per-wheel loads and forces, quasi-static
load transfer in both directions, a roll stiffness distribution $k_f$ that says
how much of the lateral transfer the front axle takes.

**Per-wheel slip angles.** Apply the rigid-body velocity relation
$\mathbf{v}_p = (v_x - r y_p,\ v_y + r x_p)$ at each wheel position. For the
front left at $(l_f, +T_f/2)$ that is $(v_x - T_f r/2,\ v_y + l_f r)$, and so on:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_{fl} &= \delta_l - \arctan\frac{v_y + l_f r}{v_x - T_f r/2},
&\qquad
\alpha_{fr} &= \delta_r - \arctan\frac{v_y + l_f r}{v_x + T_f r/2} \\
\alpha_{rl} &= -\arctan\frac{v_y - l_r r}{v_x - T_r r/2},
&\qquad
\alpha_{rr} &= -\arctan\frac{v_y - l_r r}{v_x + T_r r/2}
\end{aligned}
$$

The inner wheels see a smaller $v_x$, which is exactly the effect a single-track
model cannot represent.

**Load transfer.** The longitudinal part is the axle-level result of section 5.
For the lateral part take moments about the inner contact patch (figure b of
section 5): the inertial force $m a_y$ acts at height $h$ over a track $T$, so
the total transfer is $m a_y h / T$. Splitting it between the axles by the roll
stiffness distribution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_{zf,\text{axle}} &= \frac{m g l_r}{L} - \frac{m a_x h}{L},
&\qquad
F_{zr,\text{axle}} &= \frac{m g l_f}{L} + \frac{m a_x h}{L} \\
\Delta F_{z,\text{front}} &= \frac{m a_y h k_f}{T_f},
&\qquad
\Delta F_{z,\text{rear}} &= \frac{m a_y h (1 - k_f)}{T_r}
\end{aligned}
$$

By construction the four loads still sum to $mg$ — the unit tests check that
identity directly.

**The circularity, and how it is broken.** The loads depend on $a_x$ and $a_y$,
which depend on the tire forces, which depend on the loads. The library does
*one* pass: compute the forces on the static loads, estimate $a_x$ and $a_y$
from them, recompute the loads, recompute the forces. It does not iterate to a
fixed point, so the execution time is deterministic and a worst-case bound can
be stated — which matters more on an ECU than the last percent of accuracy.

**Yaw moment.** Summing $\mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{F}_i$ over the four wheels
gives $M_z = \sum_i (x_i F_{y,i} - y_i F_{x,i})$. Substituting the four
positions:

$$
M_z = l_f\left(F_{y,fl} + F_{y,fr}\right) - l_r\left(F_{y,rl} + F_{y,rr}\right) +
\frac{T_f}{2}\left(F_{x,fr} - F_{x,fl}\right) +
\frac{T_r}{2}\left(F_{x,rr} - F_{x,rl}\right)
$$

with $F_x$, $F_y$ already rotated into the body frame. The last two terms are
the reason this model exists: a left/right **longitudinal** force difference has
a moment arm $T/2$, so torque vectoring and brake-based yaw control appear here
and are invisible to every model above.

## 8. Tire models

**Linear.** $F_y = C_\alpha\alpha$, clipped at $|F_y| \le \mu F_z$. Useful for
analysis, wrong near the limit: the stiffness stays at $C_\alpha$ right up to
the clip.

### 8.1 The brush model, and where Fiala's cubic comes from

![Brush model contact patch](images/derivation_brush.png)

**Setup.** Model the tread as independent elastic bristles. A bristle enters the
contact patch at the leading edge $x = +a$ with zero deflection and is carried
toward the trailing edge $x = -a$. While it sticks to the road, its lateral
deflection grows with the distance travelled, so the shear force per unit length
is

$$
t_y(x) = c_p \tan\alpha\ (a - x)
$$

with $c_p$ the bristle stiffness per unit length. It can only stick while that
stays under the local friction bound. Take the usual parabolic pressure
distribution, normalised so that $\int_{-a}^{a} q_z\ dx = F_z$:

$$
\mu\ q_z(x) = \frac{3\mu F_z}{4a^3}\left(a^2 - x^2\right)
$$

**Break point.** Adhesion ends where the two curves cross. Setting
$t_y = \mu q_z$ and cancelling the common factor $(a - x)$:

$$
c_p \tan\alpha = \frac{3\mu F_z}{4a^3}(a + x_b)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
x_b = \frac{4a^3 c_p \tan\alpha}{3\mu F_z} - a
$$

Ahead of $x_b$ the bristles adhere; behind it they slide and carry only
$\mu q_z$. The lateral force is the shaded area of the figure:

$$
F_y = \int_{x_b}^{a} c_p\tan\alpha\ (a - x)\ dx + \int_{-a}^{x_b} \mu\ q_z(x)\ dx
$$

**Cornering stiffness.** At small $\alpha$ nothing slides ($x_b \le -a$) and only
the first integral survives over the whole patch:

$$
F_y = c_p\tan\alpha\int_{-a}^{a}(a - x)\ dx = 2 c_p a^2 \tan\alpha
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
C_\alpha = 2 c_p a^2
$$

**Full sliding.** The patch slides entirely once $x_b \ge a$, that is
$4a^3 c_p\tan\alpha/(3\mu F_z) \ge 2a$. Using $C_\alpha = 2c_pa^2$ this is
$\tan\alpha \ge 3\mu F_z/C_\alpha$:

$$
\alpha_{sl} = \arctan\frac{3\mu F_z}{C_\alpha}
$$

**Result.** Evaluating both integrals and writing everything in terms of
$C_\alpha$, $\mu$ and $F_z$ gives a compact closed form,

$$
F_y = \mu F_z\left[1 - \left(1 - \frac{C_\alpha|\tan\alpha|}{3\mu F_z}\right)^{3}\right]\mathrm{sign}(\tan\alpha)
$$

which expands term by term into the cubic the code evaluates:

$$
F_y = C_\alpha \tan\alpha -
\frac{C_\alpha^2}{3\mu F_z}\left|\tan\alpha\right|\tan\alpha +
\frac{C_\alpha^3}{27\mu^2 F_z^2}\tan^3\alpha
$$

Both forms make the two limits obvious: the slope at the origin is $C_\alpha$,
and at $|\tan\alpha| = 3\mu F_z/C_\alpha$ the bracket reaches $1$, so
$F_y = \mu F_z$ and stays there.

### 8.2 Pacejka Magic Formula

An empirical fit rather than a derivation — the shape is chosen, not deduced:

$$
F_y = D \sin\Big(C \arctan\big(B\alpha - E(B\alpha - \arctan B\alpha)\big)\Big),
\qquad D = \mu F_z
$$

The one thing worth deriving is the slope at the origin, because that is how the
library matches it to the other two models. Write the inner argument as
$u = B\alpha - E(B\alpha - \arctan B\alpha)$.
At $\alpha = 0$ it gives $u = 0$, and

$$
\left.\frac{du}{d\alpha}\right|_{0} = B - E\left(B - B\right) = B
$$

so $\frac{dF_y}{d\alpha}\big|_0 = D\cos(0)\cdot C\cdot\frac{1}{1+0}\cdot B = BCD$:

$$
\left.\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial \alpha}\right|_{\alpha=0} = BCD
$$

`PacejkaTire::fromCorneringStiffness()` inverts this for $B$ given a target
$C_\alpha$ and a nominal load, which is why the three tire models can be
compared on equal terms.

![Tire curves](images/derivation_tire_curves.png)

### 8.3 Combined slip

A single friction budget serves both directions. Taking the friction ellipse
$F_x^2 + F_y^2 \le (\mu F_z)^2$ and solving for the lateral force left over
once $F_x$ is spent:

$$
F_y \leftarrow F_y \sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{F_x}{\mu F_z}\right)^2}
$$

Spending half the friction longitudinally leaves $\sqrt{1-0.25} \approx 87\ \%$
of the lateral force; spending 80 % leaves 60 %.

## 9. Integrators

![Integrator convergence](images/derivation_integrator.png)

For $\dot x = f(x, u)$ held at constant $u$ over a step $h$, expand the exact
solution:

$$
x(t + h) = x(t) + h f + \frac{h^2}{2}\dot f + \frac{h^3}{6}\ddot f + \dots
$$

**Euler** keeps only the first two terms, so the error it makes in one step is
the first term it drops, $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$. **Heun** averages the slope at both
ends of the step, which reproduces the $h^2/2$ term exactly and leaves
$\mathcal{O}(h^3)$. **RK4** matches the expansion through $h^4$ and leaves
$\mathcal{O}(h^5)$:

$$
e_{\text{Euler}} = \mathcal{O}(h^2),
\qquad
e_{\text{Heun}} = \mathcal{O}(h^3),
\qquad
e_{\text{RK4}} = \mathcal{O}(h^5)
$$

Reaching a fixed end time takes $N = T/h$ steps, so the *global* error is one
power of $h$ smaller than the local one: $\mathcal{O}(h)$, $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$
and $\mathcal{O}(h^4)$. Halving the step therefore divides the error by

$$
\frac{e(h)}{e(h/2)} \approx 2^{p},
\qquad p = 1,\ 2,\ 4
$$

`test_integrator.cpp` measures exactly these ratios (2, 4 and 16) on a
quarter-circle trajectory, and the figure above plots the same experiment from
the Python port: three straight lines in log-log with slopes 1, 2 and 4.

RK4 is more than enough at a 10 ms control period; choose Euler when imitating a
fixed-point implementation on the target ECU.
